Chapter 3

Identification of Additive Systems
in the Frequency Domain





This chapter introduces a system identification method to estimate continuous-time MIMO models
within an additive structure. The formulation will closely resemble that presented previously for
time-domain identification, with the primary distinction being that the estimation problem is
formulated in terms of the FRF rather than the spectra of the input and output signals. In this
formulation, the estimation procedure can be integrated with the effective frameworks for obtaining
the non-parametric FRF dataset.  For each additive term, an output-error model structure is
utilized with a numerator represented by a matrix polynomial and a denominator defined by a
common scalar polynomial, aligning with the PFE description of modal systems. An extension is
included to facilitate the estimation of marginally stable systems

This chapter  is organized as follows:  First, Section 3.1 presents background information on
frequency-domain identification; readers with a solid understanding of this topic may skip this
section. The identification setting and problem formulation are introduced in Sections 3.2 and
3.3, respectively. Section 3.4 outlines the approach for addressing the estimation problem. Exten-
sions to the standard approach are discussed in Section 3.5, while Section 3.6 presents numerical
simulations that validate the developed method.

3.1  Background frequency-domain system identification


In frequency-domain system identification, the identification criterion is either formulated using
the spectra of the measured input/output data or directly based on the FRF measurement [52].
For steady-state signals, the estimation capabilities of the frequency-domain and time-domain
methods are equal as no information is lost when converting between domains, i.e., the mapping is
one-to-one. However, the frequency-domain formulation can yield practical advantages compared
to the time-domain method in specific identification settings.

By directly formulating the identification criteria using FRF measurements, challenging paramet-
ric identification scenarios can be effectively addressed during the non-parametric modeling phase.
One such example is the closed-loop identification setting. The open-loop plant FRF is obtained
using a non-parametric indirect method, which enables the formulation of the parametric model
identification problem based on the open-loop FRF [53]. Following this approach, a parametric
model of the open-loop system is obtained without needing closed-loop parametric identification
techniques. Another example is estimating noise models.  For periodic excitation signals, non-
parametric estimates of the noise model are obtained cheaply. These non-parametric noise models
can be included in the parametric identification criterion as weighting filters to improve the stat-
istical efficiency of the estimates [52].

Additional advantages include efficient data compression and the ease of integrating data from mul-
tiple experiments. A smaller dataset of frequency spectra can typically accurately represent large
time-domain signal measurements. Moreover, the averaging techniques used in non-parametric
modeling approaches allow large datasets from various experiments to be combined. This reduces
the noise level in the FRF and quantifies uncertainty due to non-linear distortions [52, 54]. Al-
though the frequency-domain identification framework yields some practical advantages over the
time-domain methods, it also has limitations. First, the Fourier transform is a linear operation,
which must be performed off-line.  Therefore, the frequency-domain approach is inherently un-
suited for addressing non-linearities and cannot be reformulated directly to a recursive form for
online parameter estimation. Furthermore, possible errors due to the conversion of the data to
the frequency domain, such as leakage or aliasing, will also influence the quality of the parametric
model.

The frequency-domain identification approach has gained significant popularity, particularly for vi-
brational and modal analysis of mechanical systems, where the MIMO setting has been extensively
addressed.  Typically, these methods use matrix fractional descriptions (MFD) to parameterize
the model and formulate the identification criterion as a least-squares optimization problem. A
key challenge in these methods has been the development of iterative estimation algorithms to
solve the resulting non-linear least-squares problem. Two commonly used algorithms are the
Sanathanan-Koerner (SK) method [55] and the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm [56, 57],
the latter being an improved variant of the well-known Gauss-Newton (GN) algorithm.

In the SK method, the non-linear least-squares problem is solved iteratively using a pseudo-linear
regression algorithm [58, 59], where a solution is obtained to the original non-linear problem
through a sequence of linear regressions. The advantages of this algorithm are computational
efficiency, especially for issues with a large number of parameters, and a lower sensitivity to
initialization compared to GN algorithms [60]. The main disadvantage of the SK method is that
the algorithm only reaches a stationary point of the cost function under restrictive conditions
[61, 62]. The GN algorithm is a gradient-descent method that addresses the estimation problem
through a series of local linearizations. Typically, the GN method converges to a minimum of the
cost function but can be sensitive to initialization and converges more slowly compared to SK.
Therefore, a common strategy is to combine the SK method with GN, using SK to generate an
initial estimate that GN then refines. This idea was initially proposed in [63] and has been applied
in several practical applications [24, 64].

More recently, an alternative MIMO frequency domain identification algorithm has been developed
that integrates instrumental variables with pseudo-linear regressions [65]. The SRIVC method is
derived such that upon convergence, the parameter estimates are optimal in terms of maximum-
likelihood, least-squares minimization, and instrumental variable criteria [33].  Regarding con-
vergence, the SRIVC method integrates features of both the SK and GN methods.  It employs
a pseudo-linear regression algorithm similar to the SK method while also leveraging the instru-
mental variable mechanism to converge to a stationary point of the cost function, similar to the
GN method [66]. The main disadvantage noted for the SRIVC method is that it can be prone to
ill-conditioning [67].

3.3.2  Systems acting in closed loop


The main difference with the open-loop setting is that, for systems operating in closed-loop,
the input becomes correlated with the noise disturbance due to the feedback interconnection.
Consequently, using an output-error cost function, as shown in equation (3.13), may lead to
asymptotically biased estimators [47]. Instead of employing an instrumental variable approach, as
discussed for the time-domain method in Chapter 2, the closed-loop setting is addressed during
the non-parametric modeling step.

The FRF of the plant operating in closed loop is identified using indirect FRF estimation methods
as outlined in, e.g., [53]. The parametric identification criterion is then formulated using this
open-loop FRF, eliminating the need for closed-loop parametric identification techniques. The
main advantage of addressing the closed-loop setting during the non-parametric modeling step is
computational efficiency: algorithms for open-loop parametric identification are generally more ef-
ficient than their closed-loop counterparts, and the non-parametric indirect approach only requires
a straightforward post-processing step of the measured FRF data.

3.4  Proposed solution procedure


This section outlines the method for solving the instrumental variable estimation problem. For
the considered model structure, the problem (3.9) is classified as a non-linear zero-finding problem
and generally does not have closed-form solutions.  Therefore, iterative solution algorithms are
typically used. The specific algorithm considered is SRIVC for the frequency-domain formulation,
as described in [65]. These algorithms address the instrumental variable equation through pseudo-
linear regressions, linearizing the problem at each iteration based on an intermediate parameter
estimate. This linearization allows for closed-form solutions to update the parameter estimates in
subsequent iterations. By iterating this process, an estimate of the solution to the original non-
linear problem is obtained. To facilitate the pseudo-linear regressions in the SRIVC algorithm,
it is necessary to reformulate the residual into a pseudo-linear form, which will be derived first,
followed by an introduction to the SRIVC estimation algorithm.

3.5.2  Noise modelling


When the noise disturbance deviates from a perfect white noise signal, the variance of the para-
meter estimates increases. To address potential coloring in the output noise, incorporating a noise
model into the identification criterion can enhance the statistical efficiency of the estimator. In the
time-domain method, a noise model estimation step is integrated into the estimation algorithm.
This extension estimates a discrete-time ARMA noise model based on the residuals at each it-
eration. The inverse of the fitted ARMA model is then appended to the prefilter used in the
SRIVC algorithm, compensating for the noise coloring. In the frequency domain, a noise model
can be estimated during the non-parametric modeling step. An essential requirement for this
process is that the excitation signals must be periodic. When using such excitation signals, the
corresponding FRF covariance matrix is obtained cheaply, using methods outlined in, e.g., [52,
54]. This approach offers several advantages: it eliminates the need to address a double model se-
lection problem (for both plant and noise models) and reduces the complexity of the optimization
problem.

This chapter employs the methods developed in the previous chapters to estimate modal model
structures in both the time and frequency domains. For SISO systems, the additive model structure
exactly matches the modal system representation of mechanical systems [26]. However, for MIMO
systems, the numerator matrix of each modal term in the additive decomposition consists of a dyad
between the left and right eigenvector pair [68]. The additive identification methods introduced in
the previous chapters do not directly enforce this property. To decompose the numerator matrix
in a dyad, the matrix has to be of rank 1. However, as this condition is not directly enforced
in the estimation problem, this is typically never exactly the case due to, e.g., noise, external
disturbances, or interfering residual dynamics. Therefore, this chapter introduces a method for
optimally enforcing the modal model structure in a second estimation step.

4.1  Introduction


A typical approach for estimating the eigenvector pair is to use the first singular vectors obtained
from a singular value decomposition (SVD) of the numerator matrices in the additive structure.
The matrix formed by the outer product of the first singular vectors and the first singular value
gives the best rank-1 approximation of the original matrix in terms of the Frobenius norm [69].
However, the resulting modal system is approximative and typically yields satisfactory results only
for unweighted estimation problems and systems with sufficiently spaced resonances. Furthermore,
the asymptotic statistical properties of the SRIVC-optimal parameters are lost. Therefore, altern-
ative methods for retrieving the eigenvector pair are investigated.

To directly enforce this property, a rank 1 matrix constraint could be included in the linear
regression problem, which is solved at each iteration of the SRIVC algorithm.  The resulting
optimization problem is categorized as a Rank-Constrained Optimization Problem (RCOP), which
has been extensively studied in literature due to its many applications in, e.g., signal processing,
control systems, model reduction, and system identification [70–73]. The RCOP optimization
problems are typically NP-hard to solve due to the discontinuous and non-convex nature of the
rank function [74]. Therefore, many proposed methods either employ iterative methods or solve a
relaxed problem. Examples include, e.g., in [75], where the RCOP is reformulated as an Iterative
Rank Minimization (IRM) problem, which, upon convergence, satisfies the original rank constraint.
Other directions include approximating the RCOP using a nuclear norm heuristic [76], which is
the convex envelope of the rank function.

Although RCOPs provide a solution for enforcing the rank constraint in the additive model struc-
ture in a single-stage estimation, there are some shortcomings in the context of modal identific-
ation.  Firstly, as RCOPs are NP-hard, the complexity of these optimization problems increases
substantially, making the initialization process crucial to avoid converging to local optima. Fur-
thermore, statistical guarantees, such as consistency and asymptotic efficiency, might be difficult
to guarantee for these more complex estimation problems.

Alternatively, instead of opting for a single-stage approach, the decomposition of the numerator
matrix could be achieved in an optimal a posterori parameter transformation. A method for
such a two-stage approach was introduced in [77], named the Indirect PEM (IPEM) method. This
approach was developed for settings where the desired model structure is embedded within a larger,
more easily estimated model structure. The IPEM method provides a framework for optimally
reducing the initial PEM model to align with a more complex nested structure or incorporate
difficult non-linear constraints.

The IPEM estimates are obtained as the solution to a second least-squares optimization formulated
in parameter space; that is, the parameters of the first problem are used as data instead of refitting
the measured dataset. The cost function of the initial PEM is approximated in the second stage
by weighting the least-squares problem with an estimate of the parameter covariance matrix. The
advantage of this approach is that by formulating the estimation problem in parameter space,
the number of data points that have to be fitted drastically reduces. For instance, typical MIMO
FRF datasets for motion systems contain on the order of N ≈104 to 105 data points, whereas the
parameter vectors are generally of size 102 to 103. As a result, the cost function in the second stage
tends to be more well-behaved, which makes it easier to enforce more difficult model structures or
include difficult non-linear constraints. Moreover, because the numerator matrices estimated with
SRIVC are usually close to rank-1 for the considered estimation problems, the singular vectors
obtained through SVD offer an accurate initial estimate, decreasing the likelihood of converging
to a local optimum. This enables the second optimization problem to be solved efficiently despite
the NP-hard nature of the rank condition.

4.2  Preliminaries


Many mechanical systems are accurately described by a set of ordinary differential equations with
constant coefficients [14]. The general equations of motion of a system with p inputs and q outputs
is formulated in the nodal coordinates q(t) ∈Rq as

                             M¨q(t) + D˙q(t) + Kq(t) = Ru(t),
                                                                                                  (4.1)
                               y(t) = Lq(t),

where M ∈Rq×q is the mass matrix, D ∈Rq×q is the damping matrix, K ∈Rq×q is the stiffness
matrix, R ∈Rq×p is the input matrix, and L ∈Rq×q is the output matrix. The system representa-
tion (4.1) consists of a set of coupled second-order differential equations. To simplify the analysis,
it is often useful to transform these equations into a modal coordinate system, where they be-
come decoupled. This transformation allows the system to be expressed as a set of independently
evolving modes, making the dynamics easier to interpret and analyze.

In this section, we present the modal representations of mechanical systems for two types of viscous
damping models, namely, proportionally damped and generally damped systems. Many lightly-
damped mechanical systems are accurately described using a proportional damping assumptions,
which offers advantages in model interpretability and model complexity. However, developments
in motion system design (see, e.g., [78]) necessitate more complex damping models. While general
damping models can represent a larger class of mechanical systems in a modal framework, they
come with increased complexity and reduced interpretability compared to proportional damping
models.


4.2.1  Modal modeling for proportionally damped systems

4.4  Modal model estimation


This section presents the approach for transforming the identified additive model into a modal
model using the Indirect Prediction Error Method (IPEM). The IPEM method is a two-stage
approach that initially performs a standard PEM estimation, followed by a second estimation to
either reduce the model size or incorporate more complex constraints [77]. The key aspect of this
approach is that the two-stage estimator has the same asymptotic statistical properties as the
PEM estimator that considers the reduced model structure. That is, consistency is conserved in
the second stage while the efficiency of the estimate improves due to the reduced model set size.


4.4.1  The indirect prediction error method


The IPEM method provides an optimal way of mapping the parameter vector, describing the
additive model structure, to the parameter vector describing the modal model structure. This
method is explained as follows. Consider the nested model structures A and M, with A repres-
enting the additive model structure and M representing the modal model structure, and where
M ⊂A. We are interested in estimating the modal parameter vector ρ describing a model in M.

The central idea of the indirect PEM method is to estimate a model in M by treating the SRIVC-
optimal parameter vector ˆβ describing the additive structure as data. This is achieved by solving
a least-squares optimization problem in parameter space, where the weighting is based on the
parameter covariance estimate ˆCˆβ. Weighting by the parameter covariance ensures that the cost
function of the IPEM problem and the original SRIVC cost function, when parameterized in terms
of the modal parameters, behave approximately the same [77, Interpretation 2.3]. This approxim-
ation is most accurate near the SRIVC-optimal parameter vector ˆβ. The initial modal parameter
vector ρ⟨0⟩is constructed using the SRIVC-optimal denominator coefficient and the singular vec-
tors obtained via an SVD of the numerator matrices in the additive structure. Figure 4.2 shows
the geometric interpretation of the IPEM estimation problem [77, Interpretation 2.1].

To apply the IPEM method, the following assumptions on the model structures and data are
needed.  First, the model structures are nested, i.e., M ⊂A, such that there exists a smooth
and injective map f(ρ) = ˜β that links the modal parameter vector ρ with the additive parameter
vector β in which the predictors are equal. That is if ε(tk, β) or E(jωk, β) are the residuals used
for computing the estimates in the first stage, then ε2(tk, f(ρ)) = ε1(tk, β) in the time-domain
and E2(jωk, f(ρ)) = E1(jωk, β) in the frequency-domain.  Furthermore, the Jacobian matrix
∂f(ρ)/∂ρ must have full rank for all possible values of ρ, and both model structures A and M
give parameter identifiability (see, e.g., [50, Definition 8.1]).

4.4.5  Implementation aspects


Due to the wide variation in parameter values typically encountered in mechanical systems, the
GN algorithm may experience numerical issues. To address this, the optimization problem must be
scaled so that both the entries of the Jacobian matrix and the cost function values remain within
the numerical working range of the software used for solving the optimization. The scalings are
applied as follows: rather than solving the problem (4.30) directly, the scaled version of the problem
is solved instead
                               1                                ˜ρ = arg min     (ˆβ −f(S˜ρ)) ⊤ˆC−1  (ˆβ −f(S˜ρ))   ,                 (4.50)
                            ρ    d                                ˆβ

where S is a diagonal scaling matrix that normalizes the optimization variables, and d is a scalar
scaling parameter used to scale the cost function. For the remainder of this report, the parameter
d is selected to normalize the cost function to 1, when evaluated at the initial condition ρ⟨0⟩and
the parameter scaling matrix is selected as S = diag(|ρ⟨0⟩|)−1.


4.5  Model order selection and initialization


This section addresses the problem of determining the model order and obtaining the needed
starting values for the SRIVC algorithm. The estimation problem of the modal parameters is
inherently non-convex and can, therefore, contain many local minima. To prevent converging to a
sub-optimal solution, the estimation algorithm has to be initialized sufficiently close to the global
optimum. The initialization problem becomes especially important for large estimation problems
involving many parameters, such as those for large MIMO systems.

Each proportionally damped flexible mode of the system is described by the parameters {ωi, ζi, Bi,0}.
the key property that is exploited in solving the initialization problem is that when the modal
parameters {ωi, ζi} are known, the gain matrix Bi,0 are obtained by solving a convex optimization
problem using the dataset ZN. Since the optimization is convex, a closed-form solution relating
the gain matrix to the modal parameters through the dataset can be obtained. The addition
of this intermediate optimization problem yields two main advantages. Firstly, accurate starting
values for the modal parameters {ωi, ζi} can intuitively be derived from prior knowledge of the
mechanical systems through, e.g., the FRF or FEM modeling. Similar methods for the gain matrix
are typically much less intuitive and/or accurate. Secondly, it significantly reduces the number of
parameters for which starting values must be provided.

First, we discuss the tools used for determining the model order of the modal system, i.e., the
number of flexible modes nflex and the number of rigid-body modes nrbm. After that, the approach
for initializing the denominator and numerator parameters is introduced. For relatively low-order
estimation problems, the presented methods are generally sufficient to reach the global optimum.
However, for estimation problems for which a large number of modes have to be identified, these
tools could fall short.  Therefore, an iterative approach has been used in this study, where the
starting values are progressively refined through successive iterations. This procedure is addressed
in the last subsection.

4.5.1  Model order selection

To determine the number of flexible modes, nflex, and their corresponding frequency locations, the
Complex Mode Indicator Function (CMIF) is employed [79]. The CMIF is defined as the square
of the singular values of the FRF matrix evaluated at each spectral line. A mode is identified by a
peak in the CMIF, with the peak frequency corresponding to the flexible mode’s damped natural
frequency. The mode multiplicity is determined by counting the number of singular values peaking
at the same frequency. The key advantage of using the CMIF, instead of directly analyzing the
FRF matrix, is that it reduces the order selection procedure from a MIMO representation to
a Single-Degree-of-Freedom (SDOF) representation, making it more intuitive. Additionally, the
CMIF allows for the simultaneous detection of both the mode frequency and its multiplicity. The
singular value decomposition of the FRF matrix at each spectral line is defined as

                            Gfrf(jωk) = U(jωk)Σ(jωk)V(jωk)H,                         (4.51)

where U(jωk) ∈Cq×q and V(jωk) ∈Cp×p are the singular vectors and Σ(jωk) ∈Rq×p contains
the singular values along the diagonal. The CMIF is then defined as

                          CMIF(jωk) = Σ(jωk)⊤Σ(jωk),                            (4.52)

i.e., the square of the singular values. By plotting the diagonal entries of this function in a single
figure and checking the number of peaking singular values, the number of flexible modes nflex and
corresponding frequency location is determined.  Figure 4.3 presents an example of the CMIF,
calculated for a typical motion system with p = 8 inputs and q = 7 outputs.

The rigid-body modes are represented by a single gain matrix M for the additive model structure.
In the second stage, this matrix is mapped to a modal representation, where the gain matrix M is
decomposed in nrbm dyads of the eigenvector pair {ϕ⊤l,i, ϕ⊤r,i}. The number of rigid-body modes,
nrbm, is defined as the rank of the gain matrix, i.e., rank(M) = nrbm. To determine this from the
estimated gain matrix ˆM, a singular value decomposition is performed, such that ˆM = UΣV⊤.
The number of dominant singular values in ˆM corresponds to the number of rigid-body modes in
the system. An example of a 7 × 8 motion system is shown in Figure 4.4 containing nrbm = 6
rigid-body modes.

4.5.3   Initialization of the denominator parameters

The denominator coefficients {ai,0, ai,1} for each flexible mode are initialized based on estimates of
the modal parameter pair {ζi, ωi}. Initial estimates for the resonance frequencies ωi are obtained
from the CMIF, which are accurate up to the resolution of the frequency grid. Since the frequency
locations of the peaks in the CMIF correspond to the damped natural frequencies, this method is
generally more accurate for lightly damped modes.

Estimating the damping ratios ζi is more challenging. For simpler estimation problems, initializing
with a standard damping ratio of 1-2% is usually sufficient. For larger or more complex estimation
problems, the damping ratios can be estimated more precisely from the FRF data using the
Enhanced FRF (EFRF) method, as introduced in [79]. The EFRF method simplifies the MIMO
FRF into an SDOF approximation of the mode around its resonance frequency.  The modal
parameter pair can then be obtained by applying widely used SDOF modal parameter estimation
techniques (see, e.g., [80]) to each EFRF. The EFRF for each mode is calculated by

                      EFRF(jωk) = u1(jωi)HG(jωk)v1(jωi),                        (4.71)

where u1(jωi) ∈Cq and v1(jωi) ∈Cp are the first singular vectors of the SVD decomposition
(4.51) evaluated at frequency of the mode ωi. After the modal parameters have been determined
for each mode, they are used to initialize the additive denominator coefficients according to the
relations ai,1 = 2ζiωi and ai,0 = ω2i .

4.6.2  Monte Carlo simulations


This section repeats the Monte Carlo simulation experiment from Subsection 2.7.2 for the additive
model structure. However, in this case, the modal model structure is enforced using the proposed
IPEM method. 100 different sample sizes are collected, ranging logarithmically from N = 103 to
N = 105. M = 100 Monte Carlo runs are performed for each sample size under different input
and noise realizations. The input signal u(tk) is a Gaussian white signal of unitary variance, and
output noise v(tk) is added with an SNR of 30 [dB]. The true system was defined in (2.117).
The system is sampled at a rate of fs = 100 [Hz], and the assumed intersample behavior for all
signals is a zero-order hold (ZOH). The estimators are initialized at a random system that satisfies
β0 = β∗(1 + U(0.05, 0.05)), where U(a, b) denotes the uniform distribution.The performance is
characterized by the mean squared error (MSE), as defined in (2.118).

In Figure 4.13, the MSE simulation results for the first denominator coefficient are shown, while
Figure 4.14 presents the MSE results for the (3, 3) entry of the numerator gain matrix. The
linear decay of the modal model parameters with increasing N demonstrates that the consistency
property is preserved. Additionally, the estimates for the gain matrices exhibit a slight reduction
in variance compared to those from the additive model structure, although the variance of the
denominator parameters remains unchanged.

In the modal model structure, the numerator is described by fewer parameters than in the additive
model structure, resulting in a lower variance. The denominator polynomial is represented with
the same number of parameters as before and, therefore, does not induce any change in variance.
This behavior can be further understood by examining the IPEM weighting matrix in Figure 4.12,
where the weighting for the denominator coefficients is several orders of magnitude larger than
that for the numerator coefficients. Consequently, the optimization problem tends to minimize
the model mismatch between the additive and modal model structures primarily by adjusting the
numerator parameters.

4.7  Conclusions


This chapter introduces a two-stage procedure for the estimation of proportionally damped modal
models and generally damped modal models.  First, an additive model is estimated and then
optimally mapped to a modal model using the Indirect Prediction Error Method. This method
involves formulating a second least-squares estimation problem in parameter space, where the
parameter vector estimated in the first stage serves as input for the second stage. The two-stage
method was successfully tested through a numerical example, and Monte Carlo simulation verified
the statistical properties. Additionally, initialization and model order selection procedures were
discussed.  Initialization proved crucial when considering more challenging estimation problems,
such as those involving large MIMO systems. A convex optimization problem is introduced such
that the numerator gain matrices can be obtained through a closed-form solution. As input,
estimates of the denominator coefficients have to be provided.  Estimates of the denominator
coefficients, parameterized by resonance frequency and damping ratio, can usually be derived
from prior knowledge. The presented initialization and model order procedure offer advantages
over other modal model estimation methods in the literature, primarily because the overall process
is more intuitive.
